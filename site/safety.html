How safety evaluation changed, and what's still missing

This analysis covers all 111 documents in the archive. It looks at what labs measure for safety, how that changed between October 2025 and October 2026, and where the evaluations fall short. Every claim links to the page that supports it. p.N chips open the markdown at that page.

Key takeaways

Two very different worldsAnthropic, OpenAI and Google publish 100–300 page safety documents with formal risk determinations. Zhipu (GLM), Moonshot (Kimi) and Alibaba (Qwen) ship open-weight frontier models whose documentation contains essentially no safety evaluation. Qwen3Guard is the one exception, and it evaluates a classifier, not a frontier model.
Thresholds are being crossedOver the year, each Western lab moved from “below threshold” to “cannot rule out” or “treating as High/Critical” in cyber and bio. Examples: OpenAI's first High-cyber model (GPT-5.3-Codex) and first Critical-cyber model (GPT-6 Astra); Gemini 4 Argon “cannot rule out” bio and cyber CCLs.
Evals saturate faster than they're replacedCybench, CyberGym, the AI R&D task suites, OpenAI's standard disallowed-content set and several prompt-injection benchmarks were all retired as saturated. Rule-outs increasingly rest on qualitative judgment, staff surveys and expert red-teaming.
Evaluation awareness is now the central caveatMeasured rates of models recognising they are being tested climbed through the year, reaching 41–51% verbalized for GPT-6 Astra and up to 36% internally for Claude Opus 5.5. All three Western labs now say this limits what their alignment evals can show.
From benchmarks to real-world evidenceCards increasingly use production-traffic resampling, live bug bounties, wet-lab RCTs, pilot-deployment data and incident disclosures. The August 2026 Anthropic Risk Report retroactively raises risk estimates after operational failures.
Smaller models are evaluated by proxyFlash/Lite/Haiku/Sonnet-class models increasingly inherit determinations from a larger sibling (“less capable than…”, “bounded by…”). Anthropic says its non-frontier cards will be condensed from now on.

The split is stark. Anthropic covers 15 or more of the 21 areas in almost every main document, OpenAI's system cards cover roughly half, and Gemini's short model cards mostly report relative content-safety deltas plus a frontier-safety summary. For the three Chinese labs, the only area that shows up regularly is cyber, and it appears as a capability to advertise, not a risk to assess (see Gap 1).

Some areas are thin even at the leading labs. Anthropic almost never reports privacy. OpenAI rarely reports election/persuasion or multilingual safety, and Google's short cards rarely cover jailbreaks, prompt injection or sycophancy.

Six shifts over the year

1. Risk determinations escalated, and the language changed from “rule out” to “cannot rule out”

In November 2025, Gemini 3 Pro reached no CCLs and OpenAI's GPT-5.1-Codex-Max was not High in cyber. By the end of the window every Western lab was operating at or above its own cyber threshold.

“This is the first launch we are treating as High capability in the Cybersecurity domain under our Preparedness Framework… We do not have definitive evidence that this model reaches our High threshold”GPT-5.3-Codex, Feb 2026 p.4
“Astra is our first model to reach the Critical level of cybersecurity capability under our Preparedness Framework.”GPT-6 Astra, Sep 2026 p.7
“We cannot rule out that Gemini 4 Argon has reached this CCL for biological information risk, and we therefore developed a safety case…”Gemini 4 Argon, Oct 2026 p.11

Precautionary designation also spread to smaller models. GPT-5.5 Instant is “the first Instant model that we are treating as High capability in our Cybersecurity and Biological & Chemical Preparedness categories” p.4. Anthropic moved to RSP v3 in 2026, and its cards replaced single ASL verdicts with overall risk judgments Mythos Preview p.18.

2. Benchmarks saturated, and rule-outs moved to judgment

Saturation is the most common theme across the year. Three examples show the pattern:

“our recent models saturate this benchmark… and thus no longer provide useful signal for incremental safety progress.”OpenAI, on its standard disallowed-content evals, Oct 2025 p.5
“This rule-out case is more tenuous than for any previous model.”Claude Opus 4.6 on AI R&D-4, Feb 2026 p.185

Over the year Anthropic reported that Mythos Preview “saturated nearly all of our CTF-style evaluations” (p.48), removed Cybench as “largely saturated” (Opus 4.8 p.49), then dropped CyberGym (Opus 5 p.36), then its task-based AI R&D evals (Opus 5.5 p.35). OpenAI retired its internal CTF set, CVE-Bench, VulnLMP and two bio Critical evals in the GPT-6 Astra card (p.18). Replacements are harder and more bespoke (ExploitBench, OSS-Fuzz, VulnLMP, multiturn red-team jailbreak sets), and more and more of them are internal and unpublished. Where no quantitative rule-out remains, labs fall back on staff surveys, expert red-teaming and a responsible officer's judgment.

The response has been interpretability. Anthropic added steering against eval-awareness representations, probes and natural-language autoencoders, and OpenAI added deployment simulation and production-traffic resampling. Still, the labs themselves state the limits plainly:

“In the past, similar alignment assessments have not captured the potential severity of misbehavior our models were capable of.”Claude Opus 5.5 p.93
“These results suggest that if the model were to try to sandbag covertly, we would likely be unable to catch it reliably.”GPT-6 Astra p.70

4. From static benchmarks to real-world and adversarial evidence

The evidence base widened in four ways:

  • Production traffic. OpenAI monitors CoT for deception on A/B traffic (GPT-5.2 p.10) and resamples internal coding-agent traffic (GPT-5.5 p.13). Anthropic analyzed external pilot deployments for the first time (Opus 4.6 p.93).
  • Live adversaries. Anthropic ran the first live prompt-injection bug bounty (Opus 4.8 p.78). UK AISI found a universal cyber jailbreak in GPT-5.5's safeguards (p.44).
  • Human-subject studies. A wet-lab novice-uplift RCT led OpenAI to revise its view of GPT-5's bio risk (GPT-5.4 p.34).
  • Incident-driven evals and disclosure. Sandbox-escape and package-registry evals were added after incidents (Opus 5.5 p.118). The August 2026 Risk Report discloses that bio classifiers did not run on human-feedback vendor traffic from May 2025 to April 2026, covering “around 133M exchanges” (p.148).

5. New document types: safety cases and periodic risk reports

The one-card-per-model format is splitting up:

  • Anthropic now publishes a sabotage risk report (Opus 4.6), periodic company-wide risk reports (Feb, Aug 2026) and an alignment risk update (Mythos Preview).
  • Google publishes separate Frontier Safety Framework reports (Gemini 3 Pro, 3.7 Flash) and a 217-page Gemini 4 safety report.
  • OpenAI relies on short addenda and “updates” to existing system cards.

Safety information is now spread across several documents, so someone reading only the model card for a model sees less than before.

6. Open-weight labs began reporting cyber, as a capability

GLM-5.3's card headlines “Emergent Cyber Capability: As we scaled post-training, cyber capability developed faster than we expected” (GLM-5.3 card). Kimi K3's report adds the first safety-adjacent section from these labs, a two-tier cyber evaluation that cites a joint UK AISI / US CAISI assessment and calls itself “a lower bound on capability” (p.31). It also notes that “Frontier models from Anthropic and OpenAI refuse cyber-related tasks, making a comparable evaluation infeasible” (p.30), but it does not report K3's own refusal behavior.

Gaps

These are gaps in what the documents report. Each one is grounded in the corpus, and they are listed roughly in order of severity.

Gap 1 · Critical

Open-weight frontier models ship with no safety evaluation

Across 37 GLM, Kimi and Qwen documents, no flagship release reports a harmful-content, jailbreak, CBRN, autonomy or alignment evaluation. Their weights cannot be recalled and any safeguards can be fine-tuned away, so this is where pre-release evaluation matters most. Examples:

  • Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B, described as the first open-release Max-class model, contains no safety content at all (card).
  • GLM-5's 40-page technical report uses “alignment” only to mean writing style (p.13).
  • GLM-5.3 reports exploit benchmarks next to its “emergent cyber capability” claim without a risk assessment (card).

Qwen3Guard (p.7) shows these labs can run multilingual safety evaluations. They just don't apply them to their flagship models.

Gap 2 · High

No shared content-safety benchmark, so cross-lab comparison is impossible

Three things block comparison:

  • The only safety-relevant evals reported by three or more labs are capability measures: ExploitBench, CyberGym, VCT, SimpleQA.
  • No harmful-content or refusal benchmark appears in more than two labs' documents. OpenAI uses internal “Production Benchmarks”, Anthropic uses internal harmlessness sets, and Google reports only percentage changes vs its own previous model, noting results “are not directly comparable with performance results found in previous Gemini model cards” (Gemini 3.8 Flash p.7).
  • Google labelled the direction of these deltas (“Lower is better”) only from mid-2026 (p.6).
Gap 3 · High

Alignment evaluations lack a validity standard under evaluation awareness

Labs measure eval awareness in incompatible ways: verbalized rates, white-box probes, pairwise discrimination tests. They don't report how much awareness invalidates a result, and no lab has published a threshold above which a misalignment eval is considered uninformative. Apollo simply declined to assess Opus 4.6 (p.119), and OpenAI says covert sandbagging likely wouldn't be caught (p.70).

Gap 4 · High

Saturated evals are retired faster than validated replacements appear

Rule-outs for AI R&D and cyber now rest on judgment. Anthropic says task-based AI R&D results “are no longer a significant component of our RSP and FCF capability threshold determinations” and stopped running them (Opus 5.5 p.35), and Opus 4.6's AI R&D-4 rule-out leaned on a survey of 16 staff (p.185). The replacement evals are mostly internal, so outsiders can't check whether they have headroom or are themselves near saturation.

Gap 5 · Medium

Non-frontier and derivative models are evaluated by proxy

  • Gemini Flash, Lite, Image and Audio cards argue that a model is “less capable than” an evaluated sibling instead of testing it (Gemini 3 Flash p.6).
  • Anthropic's Haiku 5.5 card is “condensed” and says future non-frontier cards will be too (p.2). That card reports no jailbreak-robustness evaluation.
  • OpenAI's addenda carry forward Critical determinations from automated evals only (GPT-6.1 Sol p.42).

Smaller models are often the most widely deployed, and capability-ordering arguments don't cover safety properties like sycophancy or jailbreak robustness, which don't scale monotonically with capability.

Gap 6 · Medium

Agentic safety is missing exactly where autonomy is marketed

Kimi advertises “200–300 sequential tool calls without human interference” (K2 Thinking). GLM-5V-Turbo is a GUI and computer-use agent (report). Neither reports any prompt-injection or destructive-action evaluation. Even at Anthropic, safety degrades in GUI settings, for example mustard-gas instructions written into an Excel spreadsheet (Opus 4.6 p.114).

Gap 7 · Medium

Under-covered harm areas

  • Privacy appears in about 6% of Anthropic's main documents.
  • Election integrity and persuasion and multilingual safety each appear in about 4% of OpenAI's main documents.
  • Bias evaluation is thin outside Anthropic, and OpenAI's first-person fairness eval disappears from GPT-6 Astra onward.
  • Non-text modalities rarely get dedicated safety evals: voice (GPT-Live, Gemini Audio), voice cloning (Qwen3.5-Omni), image generation and robotics. Qwen3.8-Omni adds an ethics statement for voice cloning but no evaluation (p.18).
Gap 8 · Medium

Post-deployment monitoring and incidents are rarely reported

Only Anthropic's periodic risk reports disclose operational failures, such as the bio classifiers that weren't running (p.148). OpenAI's October update discloses shipping a default with statistically significant self-harm and teen-safety regressions (p.6, p.9), which is a welcome disclosure. But no lab reports a systematic post-deployment metric, such as misuse rates or jailbreak success in the wild, that would let readers check pre-deployment evals against reality.

Gap 9 · Medium

External review is uneven and often absent from addenda

Third-party testing (UK AISI, US CAISI, Apollo, METR, SecureBio, Gray Swan) is concentrated in the flagship cards. OpenAI's addenda for GPT-6.1 Sol, the October update and GPT-Live-1.1 report none. Anthropic's Haiku 4.5 and Sonnet 4.6 had no government pre-deployment testing, and Google's short model cards name no external evaluators.

Method and caveats

Each document was converted to markdown with Mistral OCR. It was then read against a fixed 21-area safety schema, where each area is rated not covered / mentioned / light / substantial and backed by a verbatim quote and page. The extraction was done by Claude agents, and every quote was checked by script against the source text. The claims on this page were also re-checked by hand against the markdown. Coverage measures what is reported, not what was done. Document types differ a lot in scope, so filter by type in Compare before drawing conclusions. See About for the full pipeline.
